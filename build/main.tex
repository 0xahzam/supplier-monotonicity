\begin{abstract}
In utilization dependent interest rate models, adding liquidity can reduce a supplier's total interest income even as the supplier's balance increases. A supplier holding a fraction $\alpha$ of final pool supply keeps income nondecreasing with a marginal deposit exactly when $\alpha(1+\eta)\leq1$, where $\eta$ is the elasticity of the borrow rate with respect to utilization. When the condition fails, the supplier can instead raise its income by withdrawing, which pushes utilization further up. A protocol turns this into a design constraint by choosing, at each utilization, the largest supplier share it intends to protect. That choice caps the elasticity, and integrating the cap describes every admissible curve. Protecting the same share at every utilization gives a power law boundary, and protecting every fresh depositor gives a hyperbolic one. On a multiple kink USDT market whose parameters are drawn from a deployed configuration, the constraint does not determine the level of interest income. A compliant floor can reduce earnings, while a repair that only raises rates preserves them at a cost to borrowers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pooled lending protocols commonly determine interest rates as a function of utilization~\cite{compound,aave}. A standard design increases the borrow rate gradually below an optimal utilization level and much more aggressively above it. The steep branch improves incentives to restore liquidity under stress, but it also creates a supplier price impact problem: an additional deposit lowers utilization, which lowers the borrow rate, and the lower rate applies to the supplier's entire balance.

Consequently, a supplier may be better off withholding capital, or withdrawing it. On a lending market at high utilization, which is when the pool most needs supply, one of us found that depositing only part of the available wallet balance earned more than depositing all of it.

Existing work on these curves treats lenders as a group or the pool as a whole: it compares the rate rules of deployed protocols~\cite{gudgeon}, models lending pools formally~\cite{bartoletti}, or designs rates that maximize expected lender wealth~\cite{baude-optimal} or keep utilization stable~\cite{agilerate}. Closest to the question here, Baude et al.~\cite{baude-leverage} account for the effect of a borrower's own position size on its borrowing cost. We study the corresponding question for a single supplier.

The role of this paper is to give an exact condition under which this cannot happen, to describe the rate curves that satisfy it, and to separate what such a guarantee promises from what it costs. Section~\ref{sec:failure} works the failure through in numbers. Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:family} derive the income condition and the admissible family of rate curves it induces. Sections~\ref{sec:fixed} and \ref{sec:fresh} develop two protection guarantees and show that both are instances of the same construction, and Section~\ref{sec:minimal} gives a power law repair. Sections~\ref{sec:multi} and \ref{sec:earnings} take a market with several kinks and separate protection from pricing, and Section~\ref{sec:onchain} states the check a protocol would implement. Detailed constructions, sensitivity studies and the smooth extension are collected in the appendices.

Most of the results are formalized in Lean~4. The Lean library, the analysis code and its data are available at \url{https://github.com/0xahzam/supplier-monotonicity}, with a record of which statements are covered.

\section{The Failure Mode}
\label{sec:failure}

Consider a market with optimal utilization $O=80\%$, a borrow rate of $R_o=5\%$ at that point, and a maximum borrow rate of $R_{\max}=100\%$ at full utilization, connected by straight lines. This is a standard kinked curve~\cite{aave}, and the change in slope at the kink is large but not unusual.

Let the market hold \$10 million of supply against \$8.3 million of borrowings, so utilization is $83\%$. The market is above its own optimal level, so the protocol wants more supply. A supplier that already holds \$1 million, $10\%$ of the pool, adds \$375{,}000, exactly enough to return the market to optimal utilization. Table~\ref{tab:failure} shows the result. The balance grows by $37.5\%$ and the income falls by $66\%$, because the lower rate now applies to the whole balance. Depositing past the kink recovers only part of the loss, and as Figure~\ref{fig:failure-mode} shows, the income never returns to where it started. The same supplier does better by withdrawing about \$302{,}000, which moves utilization further from the level the protocol is trying to restore.

Two features of this example matter later. The loss comes from the sensitivity of the rate, not from the size of the deposit. The worst point is the kink, not full utilization. Both follow from the condition derived below, and neither is visible from the slope of the curve alone.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

Let
\begin{itemize}
  \item $T$ denote total supplied tokens after the supplier's transaction,
  \item $B$ denote total borrowings,
  \item $b$ denote the supplier's resulting deposited balance,
  \item $U=B/T$ denote utilization,
  \item $R_b(U)$ denote the borrow rate at utilization $U$, and
  \item $R_s(U)$ denote the supply rate.
\end{itemize}

We assume the rate is nondecreasing, continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, with $R_b(U)>0$ for $U>0$. The formulas below apply inside each smooth segment. At a kink, where the slope changes, a deposit lowers utilization, so the slope that matters is the one of the segment below. A zero base rate at $U=0$ is permitted unless a later guarantee rules it out.

Ignoring fees, aggregate borrowers pay $BR_b(U)$ in annualized interest. Distributed across $T$ supplied tokens, the supply rate is therefore
\begin{equation}
R_s(U)=\frac{BR_b(U)}{T}=UR_b(U)
\end{equation}
and the supplier's annualized interest income is
\begin{equation}
I=bR_s(U)=bUR_b(U)
\end{equation}
Income here is the annual rate at the current state, not interest already earned. Protocols usually keep a fixed share of borrower interest, often called the reserve factor. A constant share $f<1$ scales supplier income and its derivative by $1-f$ at every utilization, so it does not change the sign condition, and the analysis can ignore it. A fee that varies with utilization would have to be included in the derivative.

Write the supplier's share of final pool supply as
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\frac{b}{T}
\end{equation}
The share is measured after the deposit, at the supplier's resulting state. Equivalently $I=\alpha BR_b(U)$, so the supplier receives the share $\alpha$ of all borrower interest.

Consider an infinitesimal increase $d\delta$ in the same supplier's deposit, holding $B$ fixed during the transaction. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{dU}{d\delta}
=\frac{d}{d\delta}\left(\frac{B}{T}\right)
=-\frac{B}{T^2}
=-\frac{U}{T}
\end{equation}
Borrowings respond to rates, but not within the transaction. A deposit settles against the current borrowings, and any borrowing that the lower rate attracts arrives later and offsets part of the loss. The condition describes that immediate effect, which is the one a supplier sees when deciding whether to deposit. How far borrowers respond afterwards is outside the claim, as in Section~\ref{sec:earnings}.

\section{Supplier Income Monotonicity}
\label{sec:condition}

Differentiating $I=bUR_b(U)$ with respect to the additional deposit gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dI}{d\delta}
&=\frac{d}{d\delta}\left[bUR_b(U)\right] \\
&=UR_b(U)+b\left[R_b(U)+UR_b'(U)\right]\frac{dU}{d\delta} \\
&=UR_b(U)-\frac{bU}{T}\left[R_b(U)+UR_b'(U)\right] \\
&=UR_b(U)\left[1-\frac{b}{T}\left(1+\frac{UR_b'(U)}{R_b(U)}\right)\right]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Marginal supplier income]
For a marginal deposit made while borrowings are fixed,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{dI}{d\delta}
=UR_b(U)\left[1-\alpha\left(1+\frac{UR_b'(U)}{R_b(U)}\right)\right]
}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Two competing effects sit inside that bracket: the marginal dollar earns the current supply rate $UR_b(U)$, while the fall in utilization it causes lowers the rate applied to everything the supplier already holds. The size of the second effect is measured by $UR_b'(U)/R_b(U)$, the elasticity of the borrow rate with respect to utilization,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta(U)=\frac{U}{R_b(U)}\frac{dR_b}{dU}
=\frac{UR_b'(U)}{R_b(U)}
=\frac{d\ln R_b}{d\ln U}
}
\label{eq:elasticity}
\end{equation}
The logarithmic form on the right is what Section~\ref{sec:family} works with. Substituting it into the income derivative gives the identity this paper is built on,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{dI}{d\delta}
=UR_b(U)\left[1-\alpha(1+\eta(U))\right]
}
\label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
Since the prefactor is positive,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dI}{d\delta}\geq0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\alpha(1+\eta)\leq1
\end{equation}
and the largest supplier share whose marginal deposit remains income nondecreasing is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\alpha_{\mathrm{safe}}(U)=\frac{1}{1+\eta(U)}
}
\end{equation}
An elasticity of $9$ protects a $10\%$ supplier, $19$ protects $5\%$, and $99$ protects $1\%$. Whether a curve is too steep for a given supplier therefore depends on its elasticity, the percentage change in rate per percentage change in utilization, and not on its slope.

A finite deposit is a chain of marginal ones. If the condition holds at every point along the way, income at the end is at least income at the start. This also holds across kinks, because income is continuous there.

The condition also explains both features of Section~\ref{sec:failure}. Above the kink the slope is $m=(R_{\max}-R_o)/(1-O)=4.75$, and on a straight segment $R_b'=m$. At the starting utilization of $83\%$ the borrow rate is $19.25\%$, so $\eta=0.83\times4.75/0.1925\approx20.5$ and $\alpha(1+\eta)\approx0.1\times21.5\approx2.15$. That is more than twice the limit, so the deposit loses money, as Section~\ref{sec:failure} found. On the steep branch elasticity is $Um/(R_o+m(U-O))$. It is largest just above the kink, where the rate is lowest. There $\eta=Om/R_o=76$, so only suppliers below $1/77\approx1.3\%$ of the pool are protected. At full utilization it falls to $m/R_{\max}=4.75$, because the same slope moves a high rate by a smaller percentage. The steep branch is therefore most dangerous at its foot rather than its top. Below the kink this curve has $\eta=1$, so the condition holds again for any share up to one half and income starts to rise. That is why the income trough in Figure~\ref{fig:failure-mode} sits exactly at the kink.

Read in the other direction, the same condition prices a withdrawal. Wherever $\alpha(1+\eta)>1$ the derivative in Equation~\ref{eq:marginal} is negative, so removing liquidity raises the supplier's income. Withdrawing lowers the supplier's share and raises utilization, which on the steep branch lowers elasticity, so $\alpha(1+\eta)$ falls until income peaks where it reaches $1$. In Section~\ref{sec:failure} this is the withdrawal of about \$302{,}000, at which $\alpha(1+\eta)=1$ exactly. So the supplier the pool most needs earns more by withdrawing, and withdrawing pushes utilization further above $O$.

\section{The Admissible Family}
\label{sec:family}

The condition $\alpha(1+\eta)\leq1$ describes a supplier rather than a curve. A protocol cannot control $\alpha$, but it can decide whose deposits to keep profitable. Write $\bar\alpha(U)$ for the largest supplier share the protocol intends to protect when the resulting utilization is $U$, and call this a \emph{protection profile}. Imposing the condition at $\alpha=\bar\alpha(U)$ gives $1+\eta\leq1/\bar\alpha$, a ceiling on elasticity that we call the \emph{elasticity envelope} $E$ induced by the profile. The ceiling also covers every smaller supplier, because $\alpha(1+\eta)$ grows with $\alpha$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta(U)\leq E(U)
\quad\text{where}\quad
E(U)=\frac{1-\bar\alpha(U)}{\bar\alpha(U)}
\qquad\forall U
}
\label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
Protecting more of the pool forces a lower envelope: a target of $\bar\alpha=\tfrac12$ allows nothing beyond unit elasticity, $\bar\alpha=5\%$ allows $19$, and as $\bar\alpha\to0$ the envelope grows without bound and the constraint disappears.

During a deposit the supplier's share only grows. Under a constant $\bar\alpha$ it is therefore enough to check the final share. A profile that varies with $U$ must be checked along the path.

Since elasticity is a logarithmic derivative (Equation~\ref{eq:elasticity}), writing the constraint as
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\ln R_b}{d\ln U}\leq E(U)
\end{equation}
and integrating it bounds the curve itself. We say that $R_b$ \emph{honors} the profile $\bar\alpha$ at $U$ when $\eta(U)\leq E(U)$.

\begin{theorem}[The admissible family]
\label{thm:family}
Let $0<U_a<U_b<1$, let $R_b$ be positive and locally absolutely continuous, and let $E(U)/U$ be integrable on the interval. Then $R_b$ honors the profile $\bar\alpha$ almost everywhere on $[U_a,U_b]$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_b(U)\leq R_b(U_a)\exp\left(\int_{U_a}^{U}\frac{E(v)}{v}\,dv\right)
}
\label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
holds for every $U\in[U_a,U_b]$ and on every subinterval. Equality throughout characterizes the \emph{boundary curve} of the profile, the solution of $UR_b'(U)=E(U)R_b(U)$ anchored at $(U_a,R_b(U_a))$. Endpoint statements follow by limits where those limits exist.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\eta\leq E$, then $R_b'/R_b\leq E/U$. The left side is the derivative of $\ln R_b$, so integrating from $U_a$ to $U$ and exponentiating gives the bound. Conversely, apply the bound to a short interval $[U,U+h]$, take logarithms and divide by $h$. The left side becomes the average slope of $\ln R_b$ over the interval, and the right side the average of $E(v)/v$. As $h\to0$ they tend to $R_b'(U)/R_b(U)$ and $E(U)/U$, which gives $\eta(U)\leq E(U)$. These limits exist at every point outside a set of zero length, which is why the result holds almost everywhere. For the piecewise smooth curves used here they exist inside every segment.
\end{proof}

The next sections develop a constant share target and protection for every fresh depositor, and Appendix~\ref{app:examples} illustrates their boundary curves.

\section{Fixed Supplier Share Protection}
\label{sec:fixed}

The simplest policy is to protect the same maximum supplier share at every utilization. The profile is then a constant $\bar\alpha$, so the envelope in Equation~\ref{eq:envelope} is a constant too,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
K=\frac{1-\bar\alpha}{\bar\alpha}
}
\end{equation}
and the design rule is $\eta(U)\leq K$ everywhere.

With $E\equiv K$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:family}, the boundary anchored at the kink $(O,R_o)$ is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_K(U)=R_o\left(\frac{U}{O}\right)^K
}
\end{equation}
This power law is the equality case of Equation~\ref{eq:family}, so its elasticity is identically $K$. The ceiling $K$ comes from choosing a flat target and is not a general restriction on interest rate models.

\subsection{The Endpoint Impossibility Bound}
\label{sec:endpoint}

A rate curve must still reach $R_{\max}$ at full utilization, and fixed endpoints therefore limit how small the worst case elasticity can be. Integrating Equation~\ref{eq:elasticity} from $O$ to $1$ shows that any positive differentiable curve connecting $(O,R_o)$ to $(1,R_{\max})$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\ln\frac{R_{\max}}{R_o}
=\int_O^1 \eta(U)\,d\ln U
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
so if $\eta\leq K$ everywhere then $\ln(R_{\max}/R_o)\leq K\ln(1/O)$.

\begin{theorem}[Endpoint impossibility bound]
\label{thm:kstar}
Any positive, absolutely continuous borrow rate curve with endpoints $(O,R_o)$ and $(1,R_{\max})$ has essential supremum of elasticity at least
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
K^*=\frac{\ln(R_{\max}/R_o)}{\ln(1/O)}
}
\end{equation}
so an elasticity ceiling $K<K^*$ is infeasible whatever the shape of the curve.
\end{theorem}

The bound is tight, since the power law with $K=K^*$ reaches $R_{\max}$ exactly at $U=1$. Appendix~\ref{sec:smooth} shows that if the curve must also have a continuous derivative, no curve attains $K^*$.

Since protected share is $1/(1+K)$, the endpoints also cap the guarantee a protocol can offer without moving them,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\bar\alpha_{\max}=\frac{1}{1+K^*}
}
\end{equation}
Optimal utilization, the rate at optimal utilization, the maximum rate, and the supplier protection target therefore cannot be chosen independently of one another. For the illustrative inputs $O=80\%$, $R_o=5\%$ and $R_{\max}=100\%$ this gives $K^*\approx13.43$ and $\bar\alpha_{\max}\approx6.93\%$, so no curve through those endpoints guarantees protection to a supplier holding more than about $7\%$ of pool supply.

\section{The Fresh Depositor Guarantee}
\label{sec:fresh}

A different policy is to protect anyone arriving with no existing position, whatever the state of the market when they arrive. Suppose such a supplier deposits $x$ into a pool with initial supply $S_0$ and fixed borrowings $B$, and write
\begin{equation}
u_0=\frac{B}{S_0}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u=\frac{B}{S_0+x}
\end{equation}
for utilization before and after. Their final pool share depends only on those two utilizations,
\begin{equation}
\alpha=\frac{x}{S_0+x}=1-\frac{u}{u_0}
\end{equation}
and substituting into $\alpha(1+\eta)\leq1$ gives the exact condition along a deposit path beginning at $u_0$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{(u_0-u)R_b'(u)\leq R_b(u)}
\end{equation}

For a nondecreasing rate the left side grows with $u_0$, so the most demanding starting point is $u_0=1$, where $\alpha=1-u$. Protecting every fresh depositor from every feasible start is therefore just another protection profile, and everything in Section~\ref{sec:family} applies unchanged.

\begin{theorem}[Global fresh depositor condition]
\label{thm:fresh}
Protecting a fresh depositor entering from any initial utilization is the protection profile
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\bar\alpha(U)=1-U}
\end{equation}
whose envelope is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{E(U)=\frac{U}{1-U}}
\end{equation}
so the design rule is $\eta(u)\leq u/(1-u)$, equivalently $(1-u)R_b'(u)\leq R_b(u)$, and the boundary curve is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_b(U)=R_b(U_a)\,\frac{1-U_a}{1-U}
}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Substituting $\bar\alpha=1-U$ into Equation~\ref{eq:envelope} gives $E=U/(1-U)$, and $\eta\leq E$ rearranges to $(1-u)R_b'\leq R_b$. Since $E(v)/v=1/(1-v)$, the integral in Equation~\ref{eq:family} evaluates to $\ln\big((1-U_a)/(1-U)\big)$, which gives the stated boundary.
\end{proof}

The profile protects half the pool at $50\%$ utilization, a tenth of it at $90\%$, and only a hundredth at $99\%$. Promising little near full utilization is what lets the rule tolerate a steep emergency branch, and it is also the rule's main limitation, since it promises nothing to the larger suppliers a pool short of liquidity most needs. Figure~\ref{fig:envelope} compares the two profiles and the envelopes they induce.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{T02-envelopes.pdf}
\caption{(a) Protection profiles and (b) their elasticity envelopes. The flat target is $5\%$. Imposing both guarantees takes the larger protection profile and smaller envelope. The envelopes cross at $U=K/(1+K)=95\%$.}
\label{fig:envelope}
\end{figure}

The two envelopes cross at $U=K/(1+K)$, which is $95\%$ for $K=19$. Below that point the fresh depositor rule is the \emph{stricter} of the two and above it the flat target is, so neither guarantee implies the other.

Two further consequences separate this profile from a flat one. The first is that a fresh depositor guarantee cannot be offered by a curve through the origin. Applying Theorem~\ref{thm:fresh} on $[U_a,U]$ and letting $U_a\to0$ gives $R_b(U)\leq R_b(0)/(1-U)$, that is
\begin{equation}
R_b(0)\geq R_b(U)\,(1-U)
\qquad\forall U<1
\end{equation}
so the base rate is bounded below by the whole curve and in particular is strictly positive. The standard below kink ramp fails for exactly this reason: being proportional to $U$ it has $\eta\equiv1$, while the envelope $U/(1-U)$ is below $1$ for every $U<\tfrac12$. The example curve in Appendix~\ref{app:examples}, Table~\ref{tab:cavey-example}, starts at a positive rate.

The second is that a positive anchor below full utilization places no upper bound on the rate attainable at $U=1$ when the shape between them is unrestricted. For a flat target the available rate budget $\int_O^1 K\,d\ln U$ is finite. Here the corresponding integral is $\ln((1-O)/(1-U))$, which diverges as $U\to1$. A finite maximum above the anchor can therefore be reached by following the boundary and then holding the rate flat. Fixed kinks add restrictions, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:multi}.

\subsection{Piecewise Linear Form}

Onchain curves are piecewise linear, and the guarantee takes an exact form on them. Take a nondecreasing segment from $(u_L,R_L)$ to $(u_H,R_H)$ with slope
\begin{equation}
m=\frac{R_H-R_L}{u_H-u_L}
\end{equation}
so that $R_b(u)=R_L+m(u-u_L)$ inside it. The condition $\eta\leq u/(1-u)$ is equivalent to $m(1-u)\leq R_b(u)$, and since $R_b(u)/(1-u)$ increases along a nondecreasing segment, the binding point is the left endpoint. The segment is admissible exactly when $0\leq m\leq R_L/(1-u_L)$, and substituting the slope turns this into a rule on adjacent kinks alone,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{R_H}{R_L}
\leq
1+\frac{u_H-u_L}{1-u_L}
}
\label{eq:adjacent-rate-rule}
\end{equation}
If every segment of a continuous piecewise linear curve satisfies Equation~\ref{eq:adjacent-rate-rule}, income stays nondecreasing even for a deposit that crosses several segments, and no condition linking one segment to the next is required. A flat clamp after the final kink preserves the condition as well.

Appendix~\ref{app:examples} gives an admissible curve and follows a fresh deposit across its kinks, separating the sign of marginal income from the level of income.

\section{A Minimal Change Construction}
\label{sec:minimal}

Return to a flat target and suppose a protocol already runs a single kink model
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{old}}(U)=
\begin{cases}
R_o\dfrac{U}{O}, & U\leq O\\[5pt]
R_o+m(U-O), & U>O
\end{cases}
\qquad
m=\frac{R_{\max}-R_o}{1-O}
\end{equation}
Assume $K\geq1$ and the upper branch has negative intercept, $R_o-mO<0$, as in the illustrative curve. Below the optimum $\eta=1$ and nothing needs to change. Above it $\eta_{\mathrm{old}}(U)=Um/(R_o+m(U-O))$ is largest at the kink. For $K\geq K^*$, let $U_c$ be the first utilization above $O$ at which the power law boundary catches the original branch,
\begin{equation}
R_o\left(\frac{U_c}{O}\right)^K
=
R_o+m(U_c-O)
\end{equation}
For a feasible $K$ the natural construction follows the boundary only over that interval,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_{\mathrm{new}}(U)=
\begin{cases}
R_o\dfrac{U}{O}, & U\leq O\\[6pt]
R_o\left(\dfrac{U}{O}\right)^K, & O<U<U_c\\[7pt]
R_o+m(U-O), & U_c\leq U\leq1
\end{cases}
}
\end{equation}
preserving the curve below the kink and after the rejoin. The replacement can extend into a region where the original elasticity is already below $K$, because the power law branch runs below the original and must still catch up with it. If the original curve is admissible everywhere, no replacement interval is needed. As $\bar\alpha\to0$ the ceiling $K\to\infty$, and once $K$ exceeds the maximum original elasticity,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\bar\alpha\to0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R_{\mathrm{new}}(U)=R_{\mathrm{old}}(U)
}
\end{equation}
so the construction is better read as a constraint imposed on an existing interest rate model than as a proposal to replace it.

Figure~\ref{fig:borrow-curves} compares borrow and supply rates for $O=80\%$, $R_o=5\%$, $R_{\max}=100\%$ and $K=19$. The branch changes the path between the fixed endpoints while keeping both the kink rate and the maximum rate.

\end{figure}

Over its replacement interval this curve pays less than the baseline at the same utilization. The benefit is that adding capital cannot reduce whole position income while the supplier remains within the protected share. Appendix~\ref{app:examples} gives a finite deposit example. Preserving existing income levels requires a different pricing choice, considered below.

\section{Several Kinks and the Choice of Rates}
\label{sec:multi}

Consider a USDT market, with parameters drawn from a deployed configuration, having a $0.5\%$ minimum borrow APR, an $80\%$ main kink at $5.25\%$ APR, and a maximum borrow APR of $90\%$. Further kinks lie at $85\%$, $90\%$, $95\%$, $99\%$ and $99.5\%$ utilization, with rates specified in Appendix~\ref{app:multi}. The example lets us distinguish the protection a curve provides from the price levels chosen to provide it.

To protect both fresh depositors and existing suppliers up to a fixed share, impose the stronger promise at each utilization:
\begin{equation}
\bar\alpha_{\mathrm{both}}(U)=\max\{\bar\alpha,1-U\}
\qquad
E_{\mathrm{both}}(U)=\min\left\{K,\frac{U}{1-U}\right\}
\end{equation}
At a $5\%$ fixed share, the fresh depositor rule binds below $95\%$ utilization and the fixed share rule above it. A power law region alone does not establish both guarantees, because every retained segment must also respect the tighter envelope. Figure~\ref{fig:multi-marginal} shows the protected share and the marginal income of a fresh depositor under both guarantees.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{04-protection-and-marginal-income.pdf}
\caption{Both guarantees on the fixed kinks. (a) Actual protected share, with the $5\%$ target marked. (b) Marginal whole position income for a fresh depositor entering a pool with $1$ million USDT supply and $990{,}000$ USDT of fixed borrowing. The floor, raise only and mixed curves coincide here. Their marginal income stays nonnegative across the crossed kinks, but the baseline's does not.}
\label{fig:multi-marginal}
\end{figure}

With kinks and maximum APR fixed, the segment constraints place a floor under preceding rates. Each guarantee caps the ratio between adjacent kink rates, $r_{i+1}\leq c_i r_i$. For the fresh depositor guarantee $c_i$ is the right side of Equation~\ref{eq:adjacent-rate-rule}, and Appendix~\ref{app:multi} gives it for each guarantee. The floor is obtained by propagating the final rate backwards as $\ell_i=\ell_{i+1}/c_i$. Moving every rate to the floor can lower rates that were already acceptable. A repair that preserves them instead sets
\begin{equation}
r_n^+=r_n^0
\qquad
r_i^+=\max\left\{r_i^0,\frac{r_{i+1}^+}{c_i}\right\}
\end{equation}
where $r_i^0$ denotes the baseline. This is the smallest admissible repair that never lowers a rate, so it preserves supplier income under the same principal and utilization history at a cost to borrowers. Figure~\ref{fig:repair-family} compares these choices with a mixed repair that permits increases and decreases while minimizing weighted changes to kink rates. The recursions, objective and feasibility conditions are derived in Appendix~\ref{app:multi}.

The bound also depends on how the curve is built. On the original kinks, both guarantees with $5\%$ fixed share protection and a $90\%$ maximum require a base APR of at least $8.68\%$. Allowing unrestricted curved regions reduces the combined bound to $1.70\%$. Requiring the analytic curve also to remain above the baseline gives approximately $1.85\%$. These are different constraints, not conflicting estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:multi} derives the bounds and shows their dependence on kink spacing.

A similar restriction appears when inserting power laws around several kinks. A branch that only lowers rates after the late $99\%$ kink reaches $57.65\%$ at full utilization and cannot retain the original $90\%$ maximum. Preserving that maximum requires changing rates earlier. The analytic constructions in Appendix~\ref{sec:analytic} locate these replacement regions directly and distinguish repairs that never raise rates from those that never lower them.

\section{Income Levels and Accumulated Interest}
\label{sec:earnings}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{07-fresh-pricing-choice.pdf}
\caption{Fresh depositor guarantee only, on the stated single deposit history. (a) Accumulated interest and (b) difference from baseline. A compliant floor is not the same choice as a repair that preserves existing rates. Principal is excluded from interest.}
\label{fig:fresh-lifecycle}
\end{figure}

Whether a depositor earns more over a holding period depends on the rates they encounter and the capital they hold at each point, rather than on the sign of the deposit derivative alone. For prescribed principal $b(t)$ and utilization $U(t)$, with a constant reserve factor $f$, accumulated interest is
\begin{equation}
A(H)=\int_0^H(1-f)b(t)U(t)R_b(U(t))\,dt
\end{equation}
where time is measured in years. Comparing two curves on identical histories gives
\begin{equation}
\Delta A(H)=\int_0^H(1-f)b(t)U(t)
\left[R_1(U(t))-R_0(U(t))\right]dt
\end{equation}
A raise only repair therefore cannot reduce accumulated supplier interest under the same history, and a lower only repair cannot increase it. A mixed change depends on where the depositor spends time and how much capital is present there.

Each comparison below holds borrowings fixed and replays an identical utilization path under every curve, which isolates the effect of pricing. A curve charging borrowers more would in practice face different borrowing and a different path, so the figures show what each curve would have paid on a given history, not how the market would respond, which needs a demand model such as those of~\cite{baude-optimal,agilerate}.

The examples use a constant $5\%$ reserve factor on borrower interest, with interest paid out rather than compounded into the position. They evaluate the continuous curves and omit additional fees, incentives, losses and token rounding, so differences come entirely from the prescribed rate functions and market histories.

First take a $10{,}000$ USDT depositor in a pool with $1$ million USDT supply. They hold for one year while utilization moves linearly through $80\%$, $95\%$, $99.5\%$, $85\%$ and $80\%$ on days $0$, $90$, $180$, $270$ and $365$. Under the fresh depositor guarantee alone, the floor pays about $1{,}287$ USDT, the raise only repair $2{,}231$, and the mixed repair $1{,}680$, against $1{,}983$ under the baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:fresh-lifecycle} shows the accumulated interest over the year. All three repairs obey the same guarantee and keep the same maximum. Their different earnings come from the choice of rate levels.

Higher supplier earnings on an unchanged history are funded by borrower payments. Since this depositor owns $1\%$ of the pool, each additional unit of aggregate borrower interest contributes $0.0095$ units to the depositor after the reserve factor. The guarantee controls the response to adding capital, not whether the price level suits either side of the market.

Appendix~\ref{app:histories} considers repeated deposits, stronger protection targets and different utilization exposures.

\section{Checking the Constraint Onchain}
\label{sec:onchain}

Onchain implementations represent rate curves with linear segments rather than evaluating fractional powers or exponentials, and the flat share constraint can be checked directly in that form. For a segment from $(u_i,r_i)$ to $(u_{i+1},r_{i+1})$ with slope $m_i$,
\begin{equation}
R_b(U)=r_i+m_i(U-u_i)
\qquad
\eta(U)=\frac{Um_i}{R_b(U)}
\end{equation}
and elasticity is monotone across a positive linear segment, so checking both endpoints bounds it over the whole segment. Writing $\Delta r=r_{i+1}-r_i$ and $\Delta U=u_{i+1}-u_i$, the condition at each endpoint $(U,r)$ of the segment can be put in a form with no division at all,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
U\Delta r\leq Kr\Delta U
}
\end{equation}
which suits fixed point integer arithmetic and imposes the elasticity requirement on an otherwise standard piecewise linear interest rate model. It belongs in parameter validation rather than in the rate computation, so the hot path is untouched.

The check applies to the curve actually implemented. Replacing an analytic boundary with straight chords can violate its elasticity ceiling, and integer evaluation needs its own rounding headroom. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives the chord example and the scope of the numerical checks. A derivative condition for the continuous curve is not by itself a guarantee after token rounding.

\section{Conclusion}

The condition $\alpha(1+\eta)\leq1$ separates the supplier's pool share from the rate curve's response to utilization, and turning it into a curve constraint needs exactly one economic input: the protection profile $\bar\alpha(U)$. When the condition fails, a supplier's best response is to withdraw until it holds, which raises utilization in the state where the pool can least afford it. Everything else follows from the choice of profile, through the envelope $E=(1-\bar\alpha)/\bar\alpha$, the constraint $\eta\leq E$, and the integral bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:family} describing the whole admissible family.

A flat target gives the constant ceiling $K$, the power law boundary and the endpoint bound $K^*$. Protecting every fresh depositor gives the profile $1-U$, the hyperbolic boundary and the adjacent rate rule of Equation~\ref{eq:adjacent-rate-rule}. Neither guarantee contains the other, and imposing both takes the smaller envelope.

The guarantee does not set the price level. A compliant floor need not preserve supplier income, while a repair that never lowers rates does, at a cost to borrowers. A protocol adopting the constraint should therefore state its fixed endpoints, allowed kink changes and protection target together. On a piecewise linear curve the check runs at segment endpoints, and a curve with continuous elasticity avoids jumps in the advertised protection at the cost of headroom above $K^*$. Whether the resulting prices improve participation or market outcomes is a separate question.

\subsection{One Guarantee or Both}

Protecting both an incoming depositor and an existing holder up to a fixed share requires the stronger promise at each utilization. The required protected share is therefore the larger profile, and the allowed elasticity the smaller envelope:
\begin{equation}
\bar\alpha_{\mathrm{both}}(U)=\max\{\bar\alpha,1-U\}
\qquad
E_{\mathrm{both}}(U)=\min\left\{K,\frac{U}{1-U}\right\}
\end{equation}
For $\bar\alpha=5\%$, the fresh depositor rule binds below $95\%$ utilization and the fixed share rule above it. Adding a power law region does not by itself establish both guarantees. Every retained segment must satisfy whichever envelope is tighter there.

On a linear segment $[u_i,u_{i+1}]$, write $\Delta u_i=u_{i+1}-u_i$. The fresh depositor cap on $r_{i+1}/r_i$ is
\begin{equation}
c_i^{\mathrm{fresh}}=1+\frac{\Delta u_i}{1-u_i}
\end{equation}
Checking the fixed share condition at both endpoints gives
\begin{equation}
c_i^{\mathrm{fixed}}=\min\left\{
1+\frac{K\Delta u_i}{u_i},\
\frac{u_{i+1}}{u_{i+1}-K\Delta u_i}
\right\}
\end{equation}
where a term imposes no restriction if $u_i=0$ in the first denominator or the second denominator is nonpositive. Taking $c_i=\min\{c_i^{\mathrm{fresh}},c_i^{\mathrm{fixed}}\}$ imposes both guarantees, with the complete segment constraint $r_i\leq r_{i+1}\leq c_i r_i$.

\subsection{Rate Floors and Repair Choices}

If a market must still reach its existing maximum, limiting the increase on each segment places a lower bound on the rates leading up to it. For fixed kinks and final rate $r_n$, this bound follows by setting $\ell_n=r_n$ and working backwards:
\begin{equation}
\ell_i=\frac{\ell_{i+1}}{c_i}
\end{equation}
An unbounded cap gives a zero lower bound. Every feasible curve with these kinks and top rate must lie above the resulting floor, but moving all rates down to it can reduce supplier income even where the baseline was already compliant. A protocol that wants to preserve that income can instead require each repaired rate to remain at least as high as its baseline value.

Writing those baseline rates as $r_i^0$, the least such repair is
\begin{equation}
r_n^+=r_n^0,\qquad
r_i^+=\max\left\{r_i^0,\frac{r_{i+1}^+}{c_i}\right\}
\end{equation}
Because the baseline is nondecreasing, the backwards recursion satisfies both segment inequalities. Any admissible curve above the baseline must meet the same backwards bounds, making this the pointwise smallest repair that preserves existing rates. Supplier income cannot fall under an unchanged market history, although the higher rates increase borrower costs.

The opposite choice fixes the base and never raises a baseline rate:
\begin{equation}
r_0^-=r_0^0,\qquad
r_{i+1}^-=\min\{r_{i+1}^0,c_i r_i^-\}
\end{equation}
This is the greatest admissible curve below the baseline, but its top rate is not fixed. Keeping the original top while never raising a rate is infeasible whenever the baseline falls below the corresponding fixed top floor.

Allowing both increases and decreases gives a third pricing choice. The mixed repair in the figures minimizes $\sum_i w_i(r_i-r_i^0)^2$ over feasible kink rates with the top held fixed, using trapezoidal utilization weights $w_i$. This objective measures changes to the kink parameters. It does not optimize borrower or supplier welfare, nor is it the exact integral of squared curve error.



For the fresh depositor rule, the fixed top floor has base APR $3.47\%$, while preserving every baseline rate requires $6.39\%$. Under both guarantees the minimum base is $8.68\%$ on these kinks, and the floor, raise only and mixed repairs coincide. The numbers describe this kink grid and are not universal minimum prices for monotonicity. A repair that never raises the baseline instead reaches only $12.04\%$ maximum APR under the fresh rule and $4.82\%$ under both, rather than $90\%$. Figure~\ref{fig:repair-direction} compares the two directions under both guarantees.

\subsection{Power Law Regions on a Multiple Kink Curve}
\label{sec:analytic}

With several kinks, a rate lowering power branch may fail to catch the original curve before full utilization even when the main kink and maximum satisfy the global endpoint bound. Retaining low rates at intermediate kinks can leave too little utilization range for the final increase, so preserving the maximum may require raising rates before the offending segment.

For the constructions in this subsection, assume a continuous, nondecreasing piecewise linear baseline with strictly positive base rate, as in the illustrative curve. This ensures the running-infimum minorant remains positive. For the fixed-share envelope, a baseline with a positive-slope first segment through the origin is also permitted when $K\geq1$. When $K<1$, its running-infimum minorant is identically zero and is outside the positive-rate class.

These replacement regions can be constructed by measuring the baseline relative to the fastest rate increase allowed by the guarantee. Let $F$ be a positive boundary function on $U>0$, with $F'/F=E(U)/U$, and write $q_0(U)=R_0(U)/F(U)$. Admissibility requires $R/F$ to be nonincreasing: once the rate has fallen behind its boundary, it cannot catch up by exceeding the envelope later. The largest admissible curve below the baseline and the smallest one above it are therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
R_-(U)&=F(U)\inf_{0<v\leq U}q_0(v)\\
R_+(U)&=F(U)\sup_{U\leq v\leq1}q_0(v)
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
Values at zero are taken by continuity. The first construction preserves as much borrower pricing as possible without raising any rate. The second preserves supplier pricing by raising only what the constraint requires.

\begin{proof}
The envelope condition is equivalent to $R/F$ being nonincreasing. Any admissible minorant therefore satisfies $R(U)/F(U)\leq q_0(v)$ for every $v\leq U$, giving the first bound. The running infimum is itself nonincreasing and meets that bound. The majorant follows by the same argument with future points and a running supremum. On a piecewise linear nondecreasing baseline, the resulting curve follows either the baseline or a constant multiple of $F$. Both are nondecreasing, and the construction is continuous at contacts. Thus the bounds also belong to the rate class assumed here.
\end{proof}

For fixed share protection, $F(U)=U^K$, so the replacement intervals are power laws. On the illustrative multiple kink curve with $K=19$, the minorant first departs at $99\%$ utilization and reaches only $57.65\%$ APR at full utilization. It cannot retain the $90\%$ maximum. The majorant retains that maximum by raising rates earlier, while still preserving the original base and $80\%$ kink rate. Figure~\ref{fig:analytic-power} shows both curves and their elasticity. The formulas include interior extrema of $q_0$ on each original segment, since checking the values at the kinks alone is not sufficient for the running supremum.

\label{fig:analytic-power}
\end{figure}

To impose both guarantees, let $t=K/(K+1)$ and normalize the combined boundary at the origin:
\begin{equation}
F(U)=
\begin{cases}
(1-U)^{-1},&0\leq U\leq t\\[3pt]
(K+1)(U/t)^K,&t<U\leq1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The same construction then uses hyperbolic regions below $t$ and power law regions above it, retaining the baseline wherever contact permits. This is distinct from the fixed kink linear implementation. The combined analytic majorant has base APR approximately $1.85\%$ and keeps the $90\%$ maximum, compared with $8.68\%$ for the coarse fixed kink repair. \subsection{The Cost of Keeping the Kinks}

The combined envelope has a finite budget even though the fresh depositor envelope alone does not. With no restriction on kink placement, its fixed top base bound is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_b(0)\geq\frac{R_{\max}}{F(1)}
=\frac{R_{\max}t^K}{K+1}
}
\end{equation}
For $K=19$ and $R_{\max}=90\%$, this is $1.70\%$. It is attained by a scaled combined boundary. That curve need not lie above the baseline. Preserving the baseline raises the bound to the $1.85\%$ found above.

Figure~\ref{fig:knot-sensitivity} subdivides each original segment and recalculates the linear floor. At $64$ subsegments per original segment, its combined base falls from $8.68\%$ to $1.80\%$, approaching the continuous bound. Under the fresh rule alone it falls towards zero as more of the rate increase can be concentrated near full utilization. A finite set of kinks and the continuous admissible family are different design spaces, and their bounds should not be interchanged.

\section{Discretization and Numerical Checks}
\label{app:implementation}

One caveat applies to any discretization of a smooth boundary. Sampling the constant elasticity boundary at two points replaces it by a chord whose slope is the average of the slopes it spans, so the chord is steeper than the boundary at the segment's left endpoint. That endpoint is where elasticity binds, since the chord of a convex curve has negative intercept and $\eta$ therefore decreases across the segment, and the sampled curve is more elastic there than the boundary it was drawn from. Sampling $R_o(U/O)^{19}$ at $80\%$ and $90\%$, for instance, gives a segment whose elasticity at $80\%$ is about $67$. Figure~\ref{fig:discretization} shows this chord. A ceiling imposed on a sampled curve has to be chosen with considerable headroom, or the kinks placed close enough together that the chords track the boundary.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{A02-discretization.pdf}
\caption{(a) A constant elasticity power law and its chord between $80\%$ and $90\%$ utilization. (b) Their elasticities. Matching endpoint rates does not preserve the ceiling between the endpoints.}
\label{fig:discretization}
\end{figure}

The checks above concern the continuous rate represented by the parameters. Integer evaluation introduces small steps, for which a derivative is not defined. In the illustrative multiple kink calculation, evaluating the integer formula at all $1{,}000{,}001$ utilization ticks differs from the rational curve by less than $10^{-6}$ in decimal APR. That agreement is not a proof of exact transaction level monotonicity after all token and interest rounding. An implementation needs rounding headroom and finite increment checks at its own precision, and a shared global weight table may not express an arbitrary per market repair.

\section{Smooth Constructions}
\label{sec:smooth}

A protocol may also want the advertised protected share to vary continuously with utilization. This requires a continuous elasticity profile and imposes an additional cost beyond the deposit monotonicity constraint.

The minimal change construction is continuous in the rate but not in its derivative: below the kink the slope is $R_o/O$ while the power branch leaves at $KR_o/O$, so unless $K=1$ the two disagree, and the same mismatch occurs in reverse at $U_c$. That is worth something more than tidiness, because the protected share is computed from elasticity,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{safe}}(U)=\frac{1}{1+\eta(U)}
\end{equation}
and therefore inherits every discontinuity of it. Under the minimal change curve $\alpha_{\mathrm{safe}}$ falls from $\tfrac12$ to $1/(1+K)$ at a single point, so a supplier holding $30\%$ of the pool is protected at $U=O-\varepsilon$ and unprotected at $U=O+\varepsilon$, and the guarantee the protocol advertises jumps while the state variable it depends on moves continuously. Figure~\ref{fig:protected-share} shows the jump next to the smooth branch built below.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{A03-smooth-protection.pdf}
\caption{(a) Elasticity and (b) protected share for the standard kink, the minimal power repair and a smooth branch. The repaired curves satisfy $K=19$, but the standard kink does not. The power repair jumps in protected share, while the smooth branch changes continuously.}
\label{fig:protected-share}
\end{figure}

The integral of Theorem~\ref{thm:family} also builds curves instead of only bounding them. If $\eta$ is continuous on $[U_a,U_b]$ then
\begin{equation}
R_b(U)=R_a\exp\left(\int_{U_a}^{U}\frac{\eta(v)}{v}\,dv\right)
\end{equation}
is positive, continuously differentiable, and has elasticity exactly $\eta$. Conversely every positive $C^1$ curve through $(U_a,R_a)$ is of this form with $\eta$ its own elasticity, and a piecewise continuous $\eta$ gives the same correspondence away from its jumps. Prescribing elasticity is therefore equivalent to prescribing the rate, and the construction below works in that direction, because it makes the protected share continuous.

Removing the jump means asking elasticity itself to vary continuously. Choose a continuous profile $\eta$ on $[O,1]$ that starts at the initial value $\eta(O)=\eta_0$, stays below a ceiling $K$, and recover the rate from it,
\begin{equation}
R_b(U)=R_o\exp\left(\int_O^U\frac{\eta(v)}{v}\,dv\right)
\end{equation}
Then $R_b'=\eta R_b/U$ is continuous, the branch is $C^1$, and matching $\eta_0$ at the kink makes the assembled curve $C^1$ across the join. Appendix~\ref{app:smoothstep} carries out the construction explicitly for one such family.

The ceiling cannot be taken all the way down to the endpoint bound, however.

\begin{theorem}[$C^1$ makes $K^*$ unattainable]
\label{thm:strict}
Let $R_b\in C^1([O,1])$ be positive with $R_b(O)=R_o$ and $R_b(1)=R_{\max}$, and let its elasticity satisfy $\eta(O)=\eta_0<K^*$. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\sup_{U\in[O,1]}\eta(U)>K^*}
\end{equation}
so a ceiling $K=K^*$ is infeasible under $C^1$, while every $K>K^*$ remains feasible.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In $x=\ln(U/O)$ with $L=\ln(1/O)$, Equation~\ref{eq:endpoint} reads $\int_0^L\eta\,dx=K^*L$, so $K^*$ is the average elasticity the curve must achieve. Continuity with $\eta(0)=\eta_0<K^*$ holds $\eta$ below $K^*$ over an interval of positive length, and an average of $K^*$ then forces $\eta>K^*$ somewhere later. Feasibility for $K>K^*$ follows from the construction in Appendix~\ref{app:smoothstep}.
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:kstar} makes $K^*$ the minimum worst case elasticity and Theorem~\ref{thm:strict} makes it a minimum that is approached without being reached, since the curve attaining it is the pure power law, which is exactly the one that gives up $C^1$ at the kink.

The design choice is therefore a plain one. The power repair is the greatest admissible curve below the single kink baseline and is $C^0$, with a protected share that drops at the kink. A continuous elasticity profile gives $C^1$ and a protected share that slides, but only by spending elasticity headroom above $K^*$: for the illustrative inputs, a ceiling of $K=19$ buys a transition spread from $80\%$ to $91.9\%$ utilization. Both leave $O$, $R_o$ and $R_{\max}$ where they were.

\subsection{An Explicit \texorpdfstring{$C^1$}{C1} Branch}
\label{app:smoothstep}

For a concrete construction, we use the following family to compute the headroom required by smoothing.

Work in $x=\ln(U/O)\in[0,L]$ with $L=\ln(1/O)$ and $A=\ln(R_{\max}/R_o)=K^*L$, and let $s(t)=3t^2-2t^3$ be the standard smoothstep, which has $s(0)=0$, $s(1)=1$, $s'(0)=s'(1)=0$ and $\int_0^1 s=\tfrac12$. For $0<w\leq L$, prescribe elasticity as a ramp that finishes within the interval, followed by a plateau,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta(x)=
\begin{cases}
\eta_0+(\eta_{\max}-\eta_0)\,s(x/w), & 0\leq x\leq w\\[3pt]
\eta_{\max}, & w<x\leq L
\end{cases}
}
\end{equation}
and recover $R_b$ from it by the integral construction. Taking $\eta_0=1$ matches the ramp below the kink, so the curve is $C^1$ there, and the vanishing endpoint slopes of $s$ make $\eta$ itself $C^1$ and hence $R_b\in C^2$.

The plateau is not a free parameter, since the branch must still arrive at $R_{\max}$.

\begin{proposition}[The cost of smoothing]
\label{prop:width}
The endpoint condition $\int_0^L\eta\,dx=A$ determines the plateau uniquely,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_{\max}(w)=\frac{A-\eta_0w/2}{L-w/2}
\qquad 0\leq w\leq L
}
\end{equation}
with $\eta_{\max}(0)=K^*$ and, whenever $\eta_0<K^*$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\eta_{\max}}{dw}=\frac{L(K^*-\eta_0)}{2\,(L-w/2)^2}>0
\end{equation}
so $\eta_{\max}(w)>K^*$ for every $w>0$ in this domain. The value at $w=0$ denotes the nonsmooth limit. For a width in $[0,L]$, the branch respects a ceiling $K>\eta_0$ if and only if
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
w\leq\frac{2L\,(K-K^*)}{K-\eta_0}
}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Integrating the profile, $\int_0^w\eta\,dx=\eta_0w+(\eta_{\max}-\eta_0)w\int_0^1s=w(\eta_0+\eta_{\max})/2$, so
\begin{equation}
\int_0^L\eta\,dx=\frac{w(\eta_0+\eta_{\max})}{2}+\eta_{\max}(L-w)=\eta_{\max}\left(L-\frac{w}{2}\right)+\frac{\eta_0w}{2}
\end{equation}
Setting this equal to $A$ and solving gives $\eta_{\max}$, and differentiating gives the stated derivative, whose sign is that of $K^*-\eta_0$ because $A=K^*L$. Imposing $\eta_{\max}(w)\leq K$ and rearranging yields the width bound.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:width} is the quantitative form of Theorem~\ref{thm:strict}: the plateau meets $K^*$ only in the degenerate case $w=0$, which is the power law and is not $C^1$. The rate is available in closed form as well, with $\int_0^t s=t^3-t^4/2$,
\begin{equation}
R_b(U)=R_o\exp H(x)
\qquad
H(x)=
\begin{cases}
\eta_0x+(\eta_{\max}-\eta_0)\,w\left[t^3-\tfrac{t^4}{2}\right]_{t=x/w}, & x\leq w\\[5pt]
\dfrac{w(\eta_0+\eta_{\max})}{2}+\eta_{\max}(x-w), & x>w
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so evaluating it requires no quadrature.

Table~\ref{tab:smoothing} records the trade for the illustrative inputs, where $L=0.2231$, $A=2.9957$ and $K^*=13.43$. A wider ramp buys a gentler transition and costs protected share, and at $w=L$ the ramp fills the whole branch, the plateau is reached only at full utilization, and $\eta_{\max}=2K^*-\eta_0$, which equals $2K^*-1$ under the standing choice $\eta_0=1$. For a $5\%$ target, meaning $K=19$, the widest admissible ramp has $w=0.1382$ and carries elasticity from $1$ to $19$ between $80\%$ and $91.9\%$ utilization while still reaching $R_{\max}$ exactly at $U=1$. Figure~\ref{fig:smoothness-cost} plots the same trade over all widths.

\section{The Full Supplier Limit}
\label{app:full}

Setting $\alpha=1$ gives $\eta\leq0$. When one supplier owns all deposits, $TU=B$ and their income is $I=BR_b(U)$. Depositing while $B$ remains fixed reduces utilization and therefore income wherever the borrow rate is increasing. Equivalently, with $\alpha=1$ Equation~\ref{eq:marginal} becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{dI}{d\delta}=-U^2R_b'(U)
\end{equation}
No strictly increasing rate curve can protect this supplier. A flat rate gives equality. Figure~\ref{fig:full-supplier-income} uses the single kink example with a $1\%$ floor, a supplier initially holding all \$10 million of supply and \$9.5 million of fixed borrowing. The plotted interval remains on rising portions of the curve, and a sufficiently large deposit would eventually reach the flat floor.
